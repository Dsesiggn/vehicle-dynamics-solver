## Steering reference review

The standalone [steering tool method](STEERING.md) records the original rigid-link derivation, exact page evidence, sign conversions, source discrepancies and validation results. The steering review uses Seward Chapter 6 (printed pp.154–163 / PDF pp.165–174), Smith Chapter 5 (printed pp.60–61 / PDF pp.59–60), and the following two additional documents supplied for this tool:

- Dale Thompson, Racing Car Technology, *Ackerman? Or not? Does it matter?*, supplied `Steering_Ackerman by Thompson.pdf`, pp.1–2 visually inspected. Its Figure 1 angle formulas omit arctangent; the tool derives exact finite-angle geometry instead. Its arm-line percentage is not interchangeable with the tool's explicitly defined cotangent percentage.
- Autek, *Principles of Steering Geometry*, supplied `PrincipOfSteeringGeometry.pdf`, pp.11 and 13 visually inspected for toe sign, steering-angle difference and ideal wheel-normal intersection. Publication date/edition have not been established.

Milliken Chapter 5 §5.3 (printed pp.128–129 / PDF p.83) and Chapter 19 §§19.1–19.3 (printed pp.710–718 / PDF pp.375–379) were visually inspected for zero-slip geometry, inclined steering axes, Ackermann approximation, c-factor and measured steer-steer testing. A reciprocal-sense inconsistency between its ratio definition and approximate formula is recorded in STEERING.md; the calculator does not adopt that formula.

Seward Table 6.2, printed p.163 / PDF p.174, is an independent numerical benchmark for the ideal angle relation. No typical dimensions, steering ratio or tire slip recommendation is adopted as measured vehicle data. Tire models from Pacejka and chassis/suspension design guidance from the other cataloged books are not claimed as implemented or verified by this steering review.

### Gillespie follow-up and comparison

The supplied Gillespie copy was reviewed after the initial steering derivation. Chapter 6, printed pp.196–197 / PDF pp.212–213, establishes the low-speed idealization: negligible lateral tire force and slip, unsteered rear wheels, and all wheel-normal lines meeting at a center on the rear-axle extension. Chapter 8, printed p.277 / PDF p.293, Equations 8-1 and 8-2 give the arctangent forms and separately identify their small-angle approximations. For the project's explicitly defined transverse rear-axle-midpoint radius `R_turn`, the exact forms are

    delta_o = atan(L / (R_turn + t_f/2))
    delta_i = atan(L / (R_turn - t_f/2))

for the ordinary turn with positive inside/outside magnitudes and `R_turn > t_f/2`. Eliminating `R_turn` gives `cot(delta_o) - cot(delta_i) = t_f/L`, agreeing with the independent derivation and Seward's tabulated example. `L`, `t_f` and `R_turn` use the same length unit; the ratios are dimensionless. The approximations `delta ≈ L/(R_turn ± t_f/2)` require radians. The app evaluates finite-angle trigonometry and displays degrees. Source inside/outside magnitudes map to signed project wheel headings (+right) and then to side-dependent toe; they are not themselves signed toe values. Gillespie's axle tread becomes the nominal front wheel-center track `t_f`, not the steering-axis span or rear track.

Chapter 8, printed pp.276–278 / PDF pp.292–294, describes a translating rack, tie rods and steering arms, variation of road-wheel ratio through linkage geometry, and the approximate nature of a practical trapezoidal Ackermann linkage. Its fore/aft terminology identifies whether the tie-rod connection is ahead of or behind the wheel center. It does not supply a universal numerical correction for moving any rack longitudinally. Printed pp.279–280 / PDF pp.295–296 discuss additional toe changes when the suspension moves; those effects are outside the fixed-ride-position steering solver. Chapter 6, printed p.198 / PDF p.214, separately introduces tire slip to generate lateral force, so the current no-slip benchmark is not an optimum racing setup.

The comparison continued across the relevant passages already verified in Seward, Milliken, Smith, Thompson and Autek; it did not stop at the first usable equation. The result is scoped as follows:

| Comparison | Classification and consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Gillespie's common turn center versus Milliken §5.3/§19.2, Smith Chapter 5, Seward §6.3.1 and Autek p.13 | Agreement for the planar, negligible-slip ideal with unsteered rear wheels. This establishes the nominal benchmark; it does not replace actual linkage closure. |
| Gillespie's explicit small-angle formulas versus finite-angle trigonometry | Different approximation ranges, not conflicting geometry. The exact relation is used throughout the steering sweep. Thompson Figure 1 omits arctangent without establishing a finite-angle justification; its intended approximation remains unresolved and that expression is not used as an exact equation. |
| Practical arm convergence and rack placement in Gillespie pp.277–278, Seward pp.162–163, and Milliken pp.714–716 | Compatible descriptions of a geometry-dependent linkage approximation. Milliken's rack-location trend concerns the illustrated rear-steer layout; it is not a monotonic rule for every spatial linkage. The current rack fore/aft editor changes existing neutral joint X coordinates and recomputes rigid-link closure. Rack steering motion remains lateral +Y. No new solve law or prescribed Ackermann percentage follows from the edit. |
| Gillespie p.279's qualitative high-speed directional-response statement versus racing tire/setup discussions in Smith and Thompson | Applicability must be established before making a tuning conclusion. These passages do not provide matching tire data, loads and operating conditions for a quantitative comparison. No universal performance conclusion or preferred slip-angle distribution is adopted. A later tire-model review must resolve the relevant regime with actual tire/load inputs. |

Two further source-definition issues remain explicit. **Gillespie radius ambiguity:** Figures 6.1 and 8.2 (printed pp.197 and 278 / PDF pp.213 and 294) draw `R` from a center-of-gravity symbol to the turn center, whereas the exact denominators in Equations 8-1/8-2 require a transverse distance from the rear-axle midpoint. Those distances differ when the center of gravity is longitudinally ahead of the rear axle. The calculator uses its own unambiguous `R_turn` definition and right-triangle derivation; the intended precision of the source drawing is unresolved. **Milliken ratio ambiguity:** the reciprocal-sense mismatch already recorded in [STEERING.md](STEERING.md) remains unresolved; the explicit rack millimeters per steering-wheel revolution avoids adopting it. Neither uncertainty changes the coordinate identity used for the rack-X editor, and neither has been silently treated as a verified source equation.

This comparison provides source and geometric consistency within the stated model. It supplies no measured-vehicle validation. Real-vehicle validation still requires surveyed hardpoints, rack travel/gain and measured left/right steer sweeps at the specified ride position, with their measurement uncertainties.

## Suspension steering-axis preview audit

The [steering-axis preview method](STEERING-AXIS-PREVIEW.md) distinguishes the geometric steering pivots, the physical MacPherson strut shaft, and the wheel's complete solved rotation. Complete relevant pages were visually inspected on 2026-10-29:

- **MILLIKEN**, Chapter 17 §17.5, printed pp.632–635 / PDF pp.336–337, especially printed p.633 and Fig.17.22: the upper strut mount lies on the MacPherson steering axis while the strut shaft need not coincide with that axis. Chapter 19 §19.1, printed pp.709–711 / PDF pp.374–375, identifies the double-wishbone upper/lower ball-joint steering axis and inclined-axis wheel effects.
- **SEWARD**, Chapter 6 §§6.2.1–6.2.2, printed pp.156–157 / PDF pp.167–168, Figs.6.2–6.3: front- and side-view steering-axis definitions pass through the upper/lower outer ball joints; inclined steering can change camber and wheel height.
- **HAYNES-SUSPENSION / STANIFORTH**, “King Pin Inclination” and “Caster Angle,” printed pp.107–108 / PDF pp.97–98: separated modern upright pivots replace the historical kingpin, with their connecting line defining the axis. “MacPherson Strut,” printed p.66 / PDF p.56, describes the sliding architecture without asserting that its shaft and steering axes coincide.
- **GILLESPIE**, Chapter 7, printed pp.243–244 / PDF pp.259–260: telescopic-strut architecture combines an upper chassis mount with lower links. This is compatible with the top-mount/lower-ball definition but does not independently establish shaft/steering-axis equivalence.
- **SMITH**, Chapter 4, printed pp.41–42 / PDF pp.40–41: strut packaging and compliance discussion supplies no opposing precise axis definition in the reviewed passage. It is not used as proof of an axis equation.

Seward, Staniforth and Milliken agree on the double-wishbone pivot line; Milliken provides the precise MacPherson distinction. No governing contradiction was found in these passages. This focused review does not imply an exhaustive review of any book. Pacejka is not used for this rigid-geometry display; no tire-force model or sourced scalar angle formula is introduced. The project derives the finite rotation and connecting segment from its existing SAE XYZ inputs, so no new source-to-project scalar sign conversion is required. The existing preview already uses the solved Rodrigues rotation and spindle direction rather than a vehicle-Z yaw substitution. The new checkbox displays the current finite joint-to-joint segment, with undefined/degenerate geometry omitted, and leaves the mathematical suspension solve unchanged. A five-link virtual steering axis is outside this two-pivot reference construction. Numerical, browser and physical-validation status are recorded separately in the method document.

## Using this library for a new module

1. Identify the governing model and read the relevant passages in the supplied references. Record source ID, edition if verified, chapter/section, printed page, and PDF page; distinguish an original derivation from a sourced equation. Continue checking the other relevant supplied sources after finding a suitable method. Record which passages were actually inspected and which resources or sections were not applicable, unavailable or not yet checked; do not imply an exhaustive review.
2. Declare assumptions, valid operating range, inputs, outputs, and units for each candidate method before comparing results. Separate rigid geometry, suspension motion, compliance and tire-force regimes. Use SI for future dynamic models; convert explicitly at the millimeter geometry boundary.
3. Map source axes and signs to [the project coordinate contract](COORDINATES.md). Declare frame origin, force application points, angle definitions, and action/reaction conventions. Compare methods only after converting to common definitions and units; never mix SAE/ISO orientations or tire-load magnitudes with signed components implicitly.
4. Classify differences as resolved notation/sign/unit conversions, different assumptions or approximation ranges, or unresolved contradictions. Preserve the source evidence and explain why the selected method applies. Do not average incompatible formulas, treat a qualitative statement as a governing law, or replace an unresolved input with a convenient guess.
5. Cross-check with an independent derivation, dimensional analysis, geometric invariant, hand calculation, limiting case, reference example or measured data as appropriate. State which checks were actually performed. Keep source verification, numerical/software verification and physical validation separate.
6. Notify the user of unresolved contradictions, uncertain definitions and missing inputs that affect the derivation or result. Before dependent implementation, resolve an essential uncertainty or explicitly agree on a scoped model that avoids it; continue independent work where possible. Track remaining issues and the evidence needed to resolve them in the module documentation. Generated geometry and regression fixtures are not measured or optimized designs.

The first model using these references is the [SAE coordinate and sign contract](COORDINATES.md). The existing rigid-link Newton solver is an implementation choice; it is not presented as a verbatim algorithm from these books. Tire forces, compliance and full-vehicle dynamic integration remain future work.

## U-bar input and static layout reference

For the optional U-bar editor, the following passages were visually inspected in the supplied copies:

- **SEWARD**, Chapter 4, §4.4.1, printed pp. 111–112 / PDF pp. 122–123, Figure 4.11: U-bar layout, chassis bearings, lever joints and links; distinguishes suspension/upright and bell-crank link arrangements.
- **SMITH**, Chapter 6, printed pp. 67–68 / PDF pp. 66–67: rotating chassis mounts, bar and suspension link attachments, and bearing terminology.

These passages support the component names. The four-point, mirrored representation is an app design choice, not a sourced kinematic or stiffness model. User-entered coordinates are SAE axle-local millimeters. The only geometric conversion is reflection about the center plane, `p_right = [X, -Y, Z]`; no force/sign equation from the references is used. Straight segments join the entered points, without estimating bend radii or material properties. The bearing markers do not enforce mounting alignment, and the suspension pickup does not follow a solved body. No ARB dynamics or physical-validation claim is made.

The preview now accepts a known bar bend, lever-arm tip, and suspension pickup while the chassis bearing location remains unentered. This is a rendering completeness choice: bearing coordinates are only needed for their reference markers, not for segments between the other supplied endpoints. It is not a claim that a real bar can function without bearings. Each known point is mirrored and only endpoint-defined segments are drawn. An incomplete XYZ point is omitted rather than assigning zero or constructing a plausible location. No additional engineering model or book equation is adopted.

Verification covers nullable input persistence, malformed-data rejection, compatibility with existing imports, front/rear independence, unchanged suspension solutions when ARB inputs change, missing-bearing/partial preview behavior, and independently checked Euclidean segment lengths and reflection invariants. Synthetic test coordinates are not recommended vehicle geometry.

## Outer actuation attachment

The per-axle attachment choice and original finite-rotation derivation are recorded in [ACTUATION-ATTACHMENT.md](ACTUATION-ATTACHMENT.md). The focused comparison newly inspected these complete relevant pages on 2026-10-29:

- **SEWARD**, Chapter 4 §4.1, printed pp.90–91 / PDF pp.101–102, Fig.4.2: external spring/damper, pullrod/top-wishbone and upper rocker-arm layouts.
- **HAYNES-SUSPENSION / STANIFORTH**, Chapter 3, printed pp.78–80 / PDF pp.68–70, especially p.80: direct and rod-actuated arrangements can attach to the axle/upright or to wishbone brackets. Printed p.13 / PDF p.3 distinguishes upright and lower-wishbone direct mounts.
- **MILLIKEN**, Chapter 17 §17.1 printed pp.608–609 / PDF p.324, Figs.17.1–17.2: suspension motion paths and the A-arm's two-link/ball-joint representation; §17.5 printed pp.630–631 / PDF p.335, Fig.17.20: actual inner-pivot axes. Chapter 19 §19.1 printed pp.709–711 / PDF pp.374–375 distinguishes the ball-joint steering axis and steering-induced wheel geometry effects.
- **SMITH**, Chapter 6 printed pp.65–66 / PDF pp.64–65, Figs.34–36: lower spring attachment to a lower wishbone or hub carrier, and an upper-wishbone rocker alternative.
- **GILLESPIE**, Chapter 8 printed pp.279–280 / PDF pp.295–296, Fig.8.4: arm-end pivot arcs and the distinct knuckle/steering-arm-ball trajectory; motion-derived centers must not be replaced by anti-dive force-analysis virtual centers.

These sources agree that the attachment body depends on the actual mechanism. Their different mount examples are different topologies, not conflicting instructions to put every pickup on one particular body. The app therefore asks for upright, lower-wishbone or upper-wishbone ownership where that body is defined. A rocker wishbone carrying a spring directly remains distinct from the app's separate bell crank; an independent two-ended link requires more orientation information for an off-axis pickup. No inspected passage supplies the exact signed-angle/Rodrigues transport algorithm: that is the documented project derivation from rigid rotation about two fixed pivots.

The references do not identify the user's attachment, establish the rigidity of its brackets/bushings, or validate its actuator travel. No reviewed passage contradicts the chosen rigid-wishbone transport within its stated assumptions. Applicability to compliant or differently jointed mechanisms remains unverified. Verification status is recorded separately in the method document; this source review alone is not a software-test or measured-vehicle validation claim.
